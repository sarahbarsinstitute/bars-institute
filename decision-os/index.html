A worked example

Confidence is not the same as evidence

A software business doing $248k a month is considering an 8% price rise. The model is clear: raise. But look at what that clarity is built on.

Raising prices is worth $231,759 over two years — unless you lose more than 9.5% of customers. Nobody has measured that.

That single sentence is the output. Not the 93%, not the expected value — the fact that the entire recommendation is sitting on one number the business has never tested, and that the line is only four percentage points away from where they guessed.

It changes what you do next. You do not commit and hope; you run a limited increase on one segment, measure the actual churn, and come back. The analysis has told you exactly what to go and find out, and how much precision you need.

This is what BARS produces that a spreadsheet does not. A spreadsheet gives you a number. This gives you the number, its fragility, and the experiment that would settle it.

How it works

From a conversation to a defensible answer

No distributions to choose, no correlation matrices to configure. You give ranges instead of false precision; the engine does the rest and shows its working.

01

Pick the decision

Hire now or wait. Raise prices or hold. In-house or outsource. Five two-option decisions, each a real question rather than a calculator.

02

Give ranges, not guesses

"Revenue is probably $235k–$262k, most likely $248k." If a figure is a guess, say so — it gets flagged rather than treated as fact.

03

Run 100,000 futures

Both options face identical simulated conditions, so the comparison is like-for-like rather than two separate forecasts.

04

Find the flip point

The engine re-runs across the plausible range of every assumption to find which one the answer rests on, and where it reverses.
